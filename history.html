History

From private yards to a public institute

By the 1920s, Chinese martial arts were still taught mostly inside families, village groups, and city schools, each with its own names and loyalties. The new Republic wanted a national physical culture. Guoshu — the national art — was the name chosen for that project.

Nanjing, 1928

The Central Guoshu Institute opened in Nanjing in 1928. Zhang Zhijiang (张之江), a military officer, led it as director. The school hired established teachers rather than replacing their lineages. Its ambition was administrative: shared terms, a curriculum, graded students, and instructors who could teach outside the old master-disciple circle.

A public examination

That same year the institute held a national guoshu examination in Nanjing. Fighters met on a raised platform, the leitai, in front of officials and a crowd. The match was a statement. Martial skill would be seen, scored, and put to work in schools and the army, not only kept in the training yard.

War and the end

The Japanese invasion drove the institute out of Nanjing. It kept moving with the wartime government and never recovered a settled campus. As an institution it did not outlast the 1940s. Teachers and manuals did. Students carried the methods into later schools in mainland China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and overseas communities, often still using the word guoshu.

This page is a sketch. Dates, department heads, and the wartime route deserve a source check before anyone treats them as a finished history.
