Training

Two departments, one curriculum

The institute sorted Chinese martial arts into two departments. The Shaolin department held external methods: long, athletic, and visibly strenuous. The Wudang department held internal methods, including arts taught under the names taiji, bagua, and xingyi. The split was the school’s filing system. It was not a claim that every style began on one of those two mountains.

A student’s day

  • Empty-hand forms, learned until the shape could be shown on demand.
  • Weapons: saber, sword, staff, and spear among them.
  • Paired practice, then full-contact bouts on the leitai.
  • Ordinary physical training, so guoshu could sit beside modern drills.

What the school was for

Graduates were meant to teach. A master with a handful of disciples could not staff a national program. The institute wanted instructors for schools, police, and the army, people who could explain a method in shared language.

The styles themselves stayed older than the school. Guoshu was a way of organizing practice, testing it in public, and sending it back out through teachers. That is the part of the project that lasted.
